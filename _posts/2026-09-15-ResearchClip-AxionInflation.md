Mayday, mayday! We have inflationary news.

In our [latest paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28593), we present analytical and numerical evidence that axion inflation coupled to **massive gauge fields** could support strong backreaction while remaining compatible with the cosmic microwave background (CMB) power spectrum.

If confirmed, this could open a promising route towards a model of inflation protected by symmetry, compatible with observations, and with parameters below the Planck scale.

**The long story...**

A key development in axion inflation came with the work of Anber and Sorbo in 2010. They showed that an axion coupled to massless gauge fields can trigger an instability, producing large numbers of gauge particles with one particular helicity—a preferred handedness.

This production can become so efficient that it affects the evolution of the inflaton itself. The gauge fields act as an additional source of friction, slowing the inflaton down. This is the regime known as **strong backreaction**.

There is, however, a catch. Subsequent work showed that the resulting perturbations can be too large to agree with CMB observations. Strong backreaction might still occur on smaller scales, but making it work on CMB scales has remained a challenge.

In this work, we explored what changes when the gauge field has a mass. The mass suppresses the instability and cuts off the production of long-wavelength gauge modes. This raises an interesting possibility: could the gauge fields still slow down the inflaton without producing excessive large-scale perturbations?

Our analytical results suggest that they could. At a fixed level of backreaction, increasing the gauge-field mass reduces the power in the sourced scalar perturbations. In the heavy-mass regime, this suppression scales approximately as the inverse square of the mass-to-Hubble ratio for weak backreaction, and as its inverse cube when strong gauge-induced friction is included.

We also performed dedicated lattice simulations, finding results broadly consistent with our analytical expectations.

There is still work to do before establishing a fully viable model, but these results point towards a promising new direction.

Stay tuned!
